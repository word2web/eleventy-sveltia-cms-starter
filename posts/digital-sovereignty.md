The idea is that we’ve become too dependent on big American companies like Microsoft and Google, and that European governments need to bring their IT infrastructure closer to home.

Germany now has a dedicated team inside its digital ministry for the task. France is actively planning to [migrate government machines to Linux](https://blog.elest.io/euro-office-vs-onlyoffice-the-fork-thats-splitting-the-self-hosted-office-world/).

My main home computer is Linux and I’ve been a fan of open source for over a decade. So I’m genuinely interested in the alternatives, especially on the days when Microsoft 365 and Google’s various shortcomings drive me up the wall. (If I hear one more time that a basic Microsoft 365 task requires “a simple PowerShell script,” I _will_ scream.)

Of the options I’ve looked at, [OnlyOffice](https://www.onlyoffice.com) still feels the most mature, though [Office EU](https://office.eu), an OnlyOffice fork integrated with [Nextcloud](https://nextcloud.com), is currently the fashionable sovereign ticket, although the fallout from the decision to fork has raised some uncomfortable questions about the long-term stability of these ventures.

I’m willing to give them a try. I’m also pretty sure that smaller, agile and, crucially, younger organisations could be quite creative in their tech stack and experiment with some of the Euro offerings.

But at scale, and with organisations that already have large legacy infrastructure, I’m less convinced that unmooring from American platforms is realistic in any meaningful sense. Especially in the era of AI.

And even if all the users and data make it safely to Nextcloud, if the software sits on hardware that isn’t made in Europe, and on operating systems that are still largely Windows, Android or iOS, how sovereign is any of it?

I also don’t buy the comforting story that an open-source company based in Berlin automatically makes everything safe and cuddly.

There has clearly been a stronger incentive for European administrations to tighten their own digital arrangements since _a certain someone_ returned to the White House. 

For ordinary citizens, though, the picture looks rather different. The same governments that talk earnestly about digital sovereignty for themselves are noticeably less enthusiastic about digital privacy for the rest of us. Age verification gates, expanded scanning powers, digital identity systems. It all has a distinct “sovereignty for me, but not for thee” flavour.

In purely practical terms, I’ll believe in digital sovereignty when it’s possible to build and furnish a data centre with European-made hardware and software. 

Until then, don’t cancel your Big Tech subscription just yet.
